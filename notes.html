Isomorphic Labs: the platform bet, read closely

What it is

Isomorphic Labs is the cleanest existing example of the company this roadmap is trying to build. Not a company that discovers one drug. A company that builds the machine that designs drugs, and then points it at disease after disease.

It was spun out of DeepMind in 2021 and sits under Alphabet. The founder and CEO is Demis Hassabis, who also runs Google DeepMind and won the 2024 Nobel in Chemistry for AlphaFold 2. The stated mission is to solve all disease. The starting point was AlphaFold, but the company is no longer an AlphaFold wrapper. In February 2026 it released its own Drug Design Engine, which doubled AlphaFold 3's accuracy on the hard test, the one that measures whether the model still works on molecules far from anything it was trained on. That jump, from about 23 percent to 50 percent, is the real work. Structure prediction is easy now. Predicting whether a new molecule will actually bind is not.

The two-engine business model

This is the part to study, because it answers a question the roadmap has to answer eventually: how do you fund a platform for years before it has an approved drug.

Isomorphic runs two engines at once.

  • Partnerships for cash and validation. In January 2024 it signed Eli Lilly (45 million dollars up front, up to 1.7 billion in milestones) and Novartis (37.5 million up front, up to 1.2 billion). Isomorphic runs its engine on the partner's targets. This brings in money now and proves the platform works on real programs owned by serious pharma.
  • Its own pipeline for the real upside. Seventeen internal programs in oncology, immunology, and cardiovascular disease, with the stated goal of scaling to hundreds. This is where the equity value lives if it works.

The shape matters. Partnerships pay the bills and de-risk the story without selling the platform. The internal pipeline is the actual bet. A founder building this kind of company is really designing this dual structure, not a single molecule.

Where the moat actually is

The moat is not AlphaFold. That was the earlier lesson: structure prediction is commoditized, and anyone can run Boltz-2 for free. So what is left to defend?

Three things stacked together.

  • The engine itself, the design model that goes past public tools on the cases that matter.
  • The data. Isomorphic calls its curated life-science dataset the dataverse. Proprietary experimental data is the durable moat once the models are free, because the models are only as good as what they learn from, and good binding data is scarce and expensive.
  • The compute and the org. Massive GPU fleets running in-silico experiments at volume, and a team built to use them.

None of these alone is the company. The platform is the system. That is the lesson to carry: a founder assembles the stack, and does not fall in love with any single model inside it.

The team shows the same idea. Hassabis is the founder. The president, Colin Murdoch, came from the business side of DeepMind. The chief scientist, Miles Congreve, is a real medicinal chemist who spent his career on structure-based drug design for GPCRs. The CTO, Sergei Yakneen, is machine learning and engineering. No one person spans AI, chemistry, and building. The organization does. That combination, deep AI plus deep wet-side chemistry plus real engineering, is the thing to reproduce.

The part worth staring at

Isomorphic has raised about 2.7 billion dollars in roughly fourteen months, the last round a 2.1 billion Series B in May 2026 led by Thrive, with Alphabet, sovereign funds, and others in. It has around 379 people. It is led by a Nobel laureate and built on the most famous result in the field.

And as of the middle of 2026 it has not dosed a single patient. The first human trial, for an AI-designed cancer drug, is targeted for the end of 2026, slipped from the end of 2025.

That is the most useful fact in this whole note. The best-funded, best-staffed, most credentialed version of this exact idea is five years old and still has not put a molecule into a person. It says two things. First, the financial framing in this roadmap is correct: this is a long game, and the payoff is equity over a horizon measured in many years, not salary. Second, the clinical validation gap, the space between a beautiful in-silico design and a drug that works in a body, is still wide open. Nobody has closed it yet. Open problems are where founders live.

What it means for the plan

Isomorphic proves the thesis is real and fundable. It also marks the crowded lane. Its programs are oncology, immunology, and cardiovascular disease, the large Western markets. Nobody putting billions into a platform is aiming it at underserved populations.

That is not an accident, and it is the opening. The Salk question behind this whole roadmap, who owns the medicine, points at exactly the space the well-funded platforms are not built to serve. The move is not to out-Isomorphic Isomorphic. It is to build the same kind of platform pointed at a different problem, with data and delivery that the incumbents have no reason to chase.

Study Isomorphic as the model for how the machine is built and paid for. Do not copy where it is aimed.

Schrödinger and Iambic: two more platform bets

Why these two

Isomorphic is the loud example, but it is not the only proof that the physics-plus-machine-learning platform can be a company. Two others are worth knowing, because they sit at different points of the same idea and one of them is the direct ancestor of the whole approach.

Schrödinger: the incumbent

Schrödinger is the company that was doing this before it was fashionable. Its pitch is exactly the fusion in this roadmap: ground-truth physics-based simulation joined to machine learning. The physics half is Richard Friesner's work, free-energy methods like FEP+, turned into a product. Friesner is at Columbia, which makes this the one case study with a person the roadmap can actually reach.

Schrödinger is a public company, so its numbers are visible. It runs the same two-engine model as Isomorphic. It licenses its software to most of the large pharma companies, which brings in steady revenue, and it holds its own drug pipeline plus stakes in partner programs for the upside. Drug-discovery revenue was around 23 million dollars in the first quarter of 2026, up sharply from the year before, on top of the software business.

The lesson from Schrödinger is that the dual model is not new and not unproven. The physics-plus-machine-learning platform has been a real, revenue-generating business for years. What changed recently is that machine learning got strong enough to start absorbing the expensive physics, which is both the opportunity and the threat for an incumbent built on that physics.

Iambic: the newer, machine-learning-native version

Iambic Therapeutics is the same thesis rebuilt from the machine-learning side. Its models are built to respect the rules of physics, which makes them more data-efficient and more accurate than machine learning alone. Its structure-prediction model, NeuralPLexer, made the cover of Nature Machine Intelligence. It has signed large deals, including one with Takeda worth close to 2 billion dollars in potential milestones and a collaboration with Bayer.

Where Schrödinger started with physics and added machine learning, Iambic started with machine learning and folded physics in. They are converging on the same place from opposite directions. That convergence is the whole point of the roadmap's technical bet: the durable position is the middle, where physics and machine learning meet, not either pole alone.

What the three teach together

Isomorphic, Schrödinger, and Iambic are three versions of one company, and reading them side by side gives a clearer picture than any one alone.

  • The business model repeats. All three run partnerships for cash and validation alongside an owned pipeline for equity. This is not a coincidence, it is how a platform funds itself before it has an approved drug.
  • The moat is never the model. It is the integrated system, the proprietary data, and the organization. Schrödinger's physics, Iambic's physics-informed architecture, Isomorphic's dataverse. The public models are shared by everyone.
  • The team shape repeats. Each pairs deep machine learning with deep chemistry. No founder does both alone.

For someone with a chemical physics foundation and machine learning added on top, this is the encouraging read. The exact intersection these companies are built on is the one the major is aimed at. The task is to acquire the depth, then find the problem none of them is pointed at.

Quantum computing: what it is, and where it fits

What it actually is

A normal computer uses bits. Each bit is 0 or 1. A quantum computer uses qubits. A qubit is a small quantum system, like an electron that can sit in its ground or excited state. The difference is that a qubit does not have to be 0 or 1. It can be a mix of both at once. That is superposition, the same idea from quantum mechanics where an electron is not in one fixed place until it is measured.

The useful part shows up with many qubits together. Ten qubits hold a combination of about a thousand states at once. Fifty qubits hold a quadrillion. The space doubles with every qubit added.

The link to chemistry is the whole point. A molecule is hard to simulate on a normal computer because its quantum state also grows exponentially with the number of electrons. A quantum computer grows the same way because it is also quantum. So simulating molecules is what these machines are naturally built for. Feynman said this in 1981. If you want to simulate nature, and nature is quantum, build the computer out of quantum parts.

Quantum computing is not one job

It is easy to assume quantum computing research means building the physical machine. It is really a stack of different jobs.

  • Hardware. Building the physical qubits. Cooling circuits to near absolute zero, or trapping single atoms with lasers. This is physics and electrical engineering.
  • Error correction. Today's qubits are too noisy to trust, so many are combined into one reliable qubit. This is the hardest problem in the field right now. In late 2024 Google showed errors can go down as more qubits are added, which was a real milestone.
  • Algorithms. The math of what a quantum computer would compute. For chemistry this means methods like VQE that estimate a molecule's energy.
  • Applications. Pointing those algorithms at real problems: chemistry, drug discovery, materials. This is the top of the stack, far from the hardware. This is where a chemist works.

The well-known names are spread across these layers. Some build atoms and circuits. Some prove theorems. The chemistry work sits at the top and never touches the refrigerator.

Where it stands for drug discovery

Not far yet.

  • Google ran a real chemistry simulation on a quantum computer in 2020, but the molecule was one a laptop handles instantly.
  • Pharma is already paying in. Boehringer Ingelheim works with Google. Roche worked with a quantum company on an Alzheimer's problem. Merck, Pfizer, and AstraZeneca all have small quantum teams. Startups like Qubit Pharmaceuticals are built entirely around it.
  • No quantum computer has found a real drug or beaten the standard classical methods on a real drug-sized molecule. Not yet.

The useful signal is that pharma is building these teams before the technology is ready. They are paying now to have people in place for when it works. The job barely exists, and it is already forming.

How to treat it

The PhD goes into computational chemistry and molecular simulation, not quantum computing. The hardware and theory path belongs to physicists. The classical methods are where something real can be built in the next ten years.

Quantum computing is something to learn well, not to research. Learning it well means more than reading the news. The bar is being able to read a paper that claims a quantum computer beat a classical method on some molecule and judge whether that is real or dressed up. That takes a little hands-on once, like running a small energy calculation, not years of it.

It also helps not to wall it off. A PhD in a department where people already work on quantum chemistry on quantum computers means picking it up by being near them, without it becoming the thesis.

Why this fits a chemist and chemical engineer

The aim is to be both a chemist and a chemical engineer. The chemist understands the molecule. The engineer builds and scales the system around it. Quantum computing fits that pair, because the hard part is not only the physics of the machine. It is knowing which chemistry problem is worth pointing it at, and how to build a real pipeline around the answer.

Quantum computing researchers usually do not have a feel for drug discovery. Drug discovery people usually cannot read the quantum work. The opening is in the middle: deep classical chemistry, real domain sense, and enough quantum literacy to see the moment it becomes useful. The aim is to be ready to use quantum computing for chemistry when it works, not to build the machine.
